Do I need medical leave—or a different kind of change?

A guided reflection for clients dealing with work stress, burnout, anxiety, depression, or declining functioning. The goal is not to convince you to take FMLA. It is to help you identify what needs attention and what to discuss with your treating clinician and employer.

Important: Burnout by itself is an occupational phenomenon, not a medical diagnosis. FMLA eligibility depends on federal requirements and whether a qualifying serious health condition makes you unable to perform your job or requires qualifying treatment—not simply on feeling burned out.

Stress and burnout can feel similar—but the pattern matters.

Stress

“There is too much, and I am struggling to keep up.”

Stress often involves pressure, worry, irritability, tension, sleep disruption, or feeling overloaded. You may still feel engaged with the work and imagine that relief, resources, boundaries, or time to recover could help.

Burnout

“I am depleted, detached, and no longer functioning like myself at work.”

WHO describes burnout as chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, with exhaustion, increased mental distance or cynicism toward the job, and reduced professional efficacy. Burnout is work-specific and can coexist with a diagnosable mental health condition.

Work through the decision in four passes.

1. First: is this becoming a safety or acute-health issue?

2. What is happening to your functioning?

3. Which pattern fits best?

4. Is federal FMLA potentially available?

For the usual federal eligibility rule, check your employer/HR documentation. A common eligibility pathway is: covered employer; at least 12 months with the employer; at least 1,250 hours of service in the prior 12 months; and, for private employers, the applicable 50-employees-within-75-miles rule. Other rules apply in some settings, and state/local protections may be broader.

Prioritize immediate care, not leave paperwork.

If you may hurt yourself, cannot stay safe, or are experiencing a psychiatric emergency, seek immediate help. In the U.S., call or text 988; call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department for immediate danger. FMLA can be addressed after immediate safety is stabilized.

A medical-leave discussion is reasonable.

Your answers suggest that symptoms are interfering with work or functioning. Bring concrete examples to your treating clinician: what tasks you cannot sustain, what happens during the workday, how long recovery takes, and what has already been tried. The clinical question is whether a health condition and its functional impact support leave, intermittent leave, a reduced schedule, treatment, accommodations, or another plan.

Burnout deserves intervention—but do not use the label as the whole decision.

Consider both sides of the problem: your current health/functioning and the work conditions driving chronic stress. Discuss whether a diagnosable condition is also present. If you can still function safely, alternatives such as workload changes, boundaries, time off, treatment, or reasonable accommodations may be worth exploring before or alongside medical leave.

Start with recovery and work-design changes, while monitoring closely.

If functioning remains intact, medical leave may not be the only or first option. Consider treatment/support, protected recovery time, workload or schedule changes, clearer boundaries, and accommodations when applicable. Escalate the discussion if symptoms persist, spread beyond work, or functioning declines.

FMLA may be one practical pathway—if the health condition qualifies.

Eligibility alone does not establish medical necessity. Discuss your symptoms and functional limitations with a qualified treating professional, and ask HR/your leave administrator about certification, timing, intermittent versus continuous leave, benefits, and any employer-specific process.

Verify the leave pathway before assuming FMLA applies.

Ask HR or use U.S. Department of Labor FMLA resources to confirm federal eligibility. Even if federal FMLA does not apply, employer leave, state/local leave laws, sick leave, disability benefits, or ADA reasonable accommodation may be relevant depending on your situation.

Before deciding, ask what problem you are trying to solve.

Recovery problem

“I cannot stabilize while continuing my current workload.” Leave may create protected space for treatment and recovery when clinically appropriate.

Work-design problem

“I can work, but not sustainably under these conditions.” Explore boundaries, workload, schedule, supervision, role clarity, or accommodations.

Career-fit problem

“Even if I recover, this role may not fit.” That can be explored without forcing an immediate resignation or turning leave into a frantic job search.

Do not make leave another productivity project.

If you take leave, the primary task is recovery. Stabilize first, understand what happened, then explore career options and make major decisions from a more regulated baseline.

Stabilize→Understand→Explore→Decide

What to bring to your clinician

Function, not just labels

Describe concentration, sleep, panic, emotional regulation, attendance, mistakes, decision-making, self-care, and what happens before/during/after work.

What you have tried

Note treatment, time off, workload changes, boundaries, medication if applicable, sleep changes, and whether those measures restored functioning.

What leave would be for

Identify a treatment and recovery purpose rather than simply “getting away”: appointments, stabilization, sleep restoration, symptom management, and a return plan.

Alternatives and return needs

Ask whether continuous leave, intermittent leave, reduced schedule, or workplace accommodations are clinically appropriate and what markers would indicate readiness to return.

Scope: This tool is for psychoeducation and preparation for a clinical/HR conversation. It does not diagnose a condition, provide legal advice, or determine FMLA eligibility. Eligibility, certification, and leave structure must be confirmed through the applicable employer process, law, and qualified professionals.
